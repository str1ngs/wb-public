Buyer's Guide

Buying Guide

The three pillars: stations, banks, and chargers

These three categories all move electricity around, but they're built for completely different jobs, and the spec that actually matters shifts each time.

Power stations

Wh vs W · 230V AC output · surge rating · LiFePO4 chemistry

  • Capacity (Wh) is the tank, output (W) is the engine, and you need both to be big enough. A 2,000Wh station with a 500W output can run a laptop for days but still can't power a 1,800W induction hob -- the tank is huge, the engine isn't.
  • 230V AC output is what makes these different from a power bank. A real household socket built into the unit, not just USB ports, so anything you'd normally plug into a wall works here too.
  • Surge rating matters more than the running wattage for anything with a motor or compressor. A mini-fridge might only draw 80W running, but its compressor can briefly demand 200-300W just to start. A station's continuous output rating won't show you this -- check the separate surge/peak spec.
  • LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) is the chemistry almost every serious station uses now, over older lithium-ion. It's heavier for the same capacity, but rated for roughly 3,000+ charge cycles instead of 500-1,000, and it doesn't share lithium-ion's thermal runaway risk -- worth knowing if this is going near where you sleep.

Power banks

mAh-to-Wh math · cell voltage · pocket portability

  • Power banks are rated in mAh, but Wh is what actually lets you compare them to anything else. Convert with: Wh = (mAh × 3.7V) / 1,000. A 20,000mAh bank works out to roughly 74Wh -- now you can compare it directly against a power station's Wh rating, or figure out how many phone charges it's really good for.
  • That 3.7V is the internal cell voltage, not what comes out of the USB port. The bank's own electronics step it up to 5V, 9V, or 20V depending on the port and what's plugged in, and that conversion isn't 100% efficient -- see the runtime formula below for why the real-world number is always a bit lower than the sticker math.
  • Airlines cap carry-on power banks at 100Wh (some allow up to 160Wh with approval). That mAh-to-Wh formula is exactly how they check -- a 27,000mAh bank is right at the edge of that limit, worth converting before you fly with one.
  • Portability is the entire point of this category, so weight and physical size matter as much as capacity here in a way they don't for a station that mostly sits at home.

High-draw chargers

GaN technology · multi-port wattage splitting · PD 3.1 / PPS

  • Chargers have no battery at all -- they're pure pass-through, converting wall power into the right voltage for whatever's plugged in. That's the whole category in one sentence: no capacity spec, no runtime, just how much power it can push and to how many things at once.
  • GaN (gallium nitride) is why modern chargers got small. It replaced silicon in the internal transistors, runs cooler at high power, and is the reason a 100W charger today can fit in a jacket pocket instead of needing its own brick.
  • Multi-port wattage splitting means the number on the box is a ceiling, not a guarantee per port. A "100W" charger with three ports might do 100W to one device alone, but drop to 65W + 20W + 15W the moment all three are plugged in -- check the manufacturer's port-by-port breakdown, not just the headline number.
  • PD 3.1 and PPS are the protocols that negotiate exactly how much power your device actually gets. PD 3.1 extends fast charging up to 240W for laptops; PPS (Programmable Power Supply) lets a phone request very specific voltage steps for faster, cooler charging. A device and charger both need to support the same protocol for any of this to kick in -- the wattage number alone doesn't guarantee it.
