How to Choose a Portable Power Station

Every power station listing throws two numbers at you — watt-hours (Wh) and watts (W) — and most buyers have no idea which one actually matters for their situation. Here's the short version.

Capacity (Wh) is how long. Output (W) is what.

Watt-hours tell you total stored energy — think of it as the size of the fuel tank. Watts tell you how much power the unit can deliver at once — think of it as the size of the engine. You need enough of both, and they solve different problems.

Sizing for output (W)

Add up the running wattage of everything you'll plug in simultaneously, then check that number against the station's continuous output rating. Motors (fridges, power tools, air conditioners) also have a surge/startup wattage that briefly spikes 2–3x higher than their running wattage — check the station's surge rating too, not just continuous.

  • Laptop: ~60W running
  • Mini-fridge: ~100–200W running, 400–600W surge
  • CPAP machine: ~30–60W
  • Microwave: ~1,000–1,500W running
  • Window AC unit: ~900–1,500W running, up to 3x that in surge
  • Central AC / well pump / dryer: typically needs a 240V unit like the Anker SOLIX F3800 or EcoFlow DELTA Pro 3

Sizing for capacity (Wh)

Divide the station's Wh rating by the wattage of what you're running to estimate runtime: a 1,000Wh station running a 100W mini-fridge gives roughly 10 hours (accounting for ~85–90% real-world efficiency, a bit less). For multi-day outages, add up your essential daily watt-hour usage and multiply by the number of days you want covered.

Rule of thumb: for weekend camping or a laptop-and-lights emergency kit, 500–1,000Wh is plenty. For a fridge plus basics during a 1–2 day outage, look at 2,000–3,000Wh. For whole-home, multi-day backup, you're in expandable-battery territory — 4,000Wh and up, like our top-rated F3800.

Solar input: more than you think you need

If you plan to recharge from solar during an outage, your panel wattage should comfortably exceed the station's rated solar input for the fastest recharge, and you should expect real-world solar input to run 60–80% of the panel's rated wattage depending on angle, cloud cover, and panel age.

Battery chemistry: LiFePO4 is the current standard

Nearly every unit on this site uses LiFePO4 (LFP) cells rather than older NMC lithium-ion chemistry. LFP cycles 4–10x longer (often 3,000–6,000+ cycles vs. 500–1,000 for NMC), runs cooler, and is significantly less prone to thermal runaway — worth confirming on any unit you're considering that isn't covered here.
